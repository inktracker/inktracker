Pricing

Build a screen printing price chart that already covers your overhead

By Joe, Founder, InkTracker · July 1, 2026 · 6 min read

A price chart is just your price-per-print, answered ahead of time. Instead of doing the math fresh on every quote, you build a grid once — colors down one side, quantities across the top — and every cell holds the per-print price for that combination. Then quoting is lookup, not arithmetic.

The problem is how most charts get built: straight from print cost. You figure out what it costs to push ink onto a shirt, add a markup, and fill in the grid. It looks complete. But it's missing the biggest bill you pay every month — the one that shows up whether or not the press runs. Your rent doesn't care how many shirts you printed. Neither does your power bill, your insurance, your software, your loan payment, or the paycheck you're supposed to take home.

Spread your overhead across every print

Overhead is every cost that isn't the blank or the ink: rent, utilities, insurance, software subscriptions, equipment loans, and — this is the one shops forget — your own pay. If you don't build your wage into the chart, every job silently underpays you and the shop looks more profitable than it is.

The move is simple: add up your monthly overhead, divide by how many prints you do in a month, and you get an overhead-per-print number. That's the slice of the rent each print has to carry. Add it to your direct print cost before you apply margin, and suddenly every cell in the grid is pulling its weight on the fixed bills.

The overhead math, in one line

Monthly overhead ÷ prints per month = overhead added to every print. If you spend $6,000 a month to keep the doors open and you do 3,000 prints, every print carries $2.00 of overhead before ink even enters the picture.

Margin isn't markup (and the difference is real money)

This is the mistake that costs shops the most, because it hides inside a number that sounds right. "I mark everything up 50%" feels like a 50% margin. It isn't.

Markup is added to your cost. Margin is a slice of your price. If a print costs you $6 and you mark it up 50%, you charge $9 — but your profit is $3 out of a $9 price, which is only a 33% margin. You thought you were keeping half; you're keeping a third. Every cell in a chart built on markup is quietly thinner than you believe.

Price by margin instead, and each cell is price = cost ÷ (1 − margin). Want a true 50% margin on that $6 cost? 6 ÷ (1 − 0.50) = $12, not $9. The formula guarantees the profit you actually decided to keep, on every square of the grid, instead of leaving it to a markup number that rounds down your paycheck. The note under the calculator shows the markup-equivalent of whatever margin you pick, so you can see the gap for yourself.
